EU Cybersecurity Rules: What Companies Need to Know

Sep 19, 2026 · 4 min read · Purple Lotus

For years, companies have relied on security practices such as ISO 27001, NIST frameworks, vulnerability scanning, penetration testing, and incident response to protect their systems.

These practices are still important.

But the European Union is changing the way cybersecurity is treated.

From Security Practices to Regulatory Requirements

Newer EU regulations such as the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), NIS2, GDPR, and DORA introduce specific cybersecurity responsibilities for organizations and products within their respective scopes.

The focus is moving beyond:

“Do we have security controls?”

towards:

“Can we continuously identify, manage, respond to, and demonstrate how we handle cybersecurity risks?”

What the CRA Changes

The Cyber Resilience Act is particularly important for companies developing or selling products with digital elements.

It introduces requirements around areas such as:

  • Secure product development
  • Cybersecurity risk management
  • Vulnerability handling
  • Security updates
  • Product documentation
  • Vulnerability and incident reporting

This means cybersecurity becomes part of the product lifecycle, rather than something addressed only after a vulnerability is discovered.

Why Software Supply Chains Matter

Modern applications depend on hundreds of third-party and open-source components.

An SBOM (Software Bill of Materials) helps organizations understand what components exist inside their software.

But knowing what you use is only the beginning.

Companies also need to know:

  • Is one of these components vulnerable?
  • Is it being actively exploited?
  • Does it affect our product?
  • What action have we taken?

From Vulnerability Alerts to Continuous Visibility

A typical vulnerability workflow can generate large numbers of alerts:

Vulnerability discovered
        ↓
Scanner detects it
        ↓
Ticket created
        ↓
Developer investigates
        ↓
More vulnerabilities arrive

The challenge is determining which vulnerabilities require immediate attention.

Threat-intelligence sources such as the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) Catalog can provide additional context by identifying vulnerabilities known to be exploited in the wild.

Combining this information with software inventory or SBOM data allows organizations to focus on vulnerabilities that actually affect their products.

What Companies Should Do

Companies preparing for the evolving EU cybersecurity landscape should:

  • Understand which regulations apply to them.
  • Maintain an accurate software and product inventory.
  • Use SBOMs where appropriate.
  • Continuously monitor vulnerabilities.
  • Track actively exploited vulnerabilities.
  • Establish clear response and escalation processes.
  • Maintain evidence of security and remediation activities.

Where Purple Lotus Fits

By connecting software inventories, SBOMs, vulnerability intelligence, and response workflows, organizations can build a more structured approach to modern cybersecurity.

Security is no longer just about finding vulnerabilities. It is about knowing which vulnerabilities matter, responding to them, and maintaining visibility throughout the product lifecycle.
